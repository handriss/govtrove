Searched a Solicitation Number and Found Nothing? Here’s Why

Someone forwards you a solicitation number. A teaming partner mentions one on a call. You find it referenced in an old email. You paste it into SAM.gov — and get nothing back.

The instinct is to blame the formatting. Should the dashes be there? Is it case-sensitive? Did you transcribe a zero as an O? Almost always, none of that is the problem. Here is what actually happened, and what to do about it.

The most likely answer: it already closed

Federal solicitations are open for far less time than most people expect. Looking at every notice in our index with both a posted date and a response deadline over the past twelve months:

  • The median solicitation is open for 12 days.
  • A quarter of them close within 8 days of being posted.
  • Three quarters close within 16 days.

That is the whole story for most failed lookups. A solicitation number that reaches you second-hand — through a partner, a newsletter, a forwarded email, a conversation — has usually spent several of those days in transit. By the time you search it, the window has closed.

If you are searching for a specific solicitation number, you are already reacting to something that happened days ago. The median window is 12 days, and you did not start on day one.

Closed does not mean deleted

When a solicitation passes its response deadline, SAM.gov archives the notice rather than removing it. The record still exists — but archived notices are excluded from searches of currently open opportunities, which is what you get by default on SAM.gov and on GovTrove alike.

This is the right default for anyone trying to find work they can still bid on. It is the wrong default when you are chasing one specific number, which is why the search comes back empty instead of telling you the thing you wanted exists but has closed.

Amendments create duplicate entries

SAM.gov issues a new notice ID for every amendment. A solicitation amended four times occupies five separate entries, each with its own ID, all carrying the same solicitation number.

Nearly half — about 46% — of the currently open entries in our index share a solicitation number with another open entry. Two consequences follow:

  • Searching a solicitation number may return several results that look like duplicates. They are not duplicates; they are versions. The one with the most recent posted date is current.
  • A link you bookmarked earlier may point at a superseded version. The amendment that replaced it lives at a different notice ID.

Formatting is almost never the cause

On GovTrove, solicitation numbers are normalized before matching. All of these return the same opportunity:

  • FA2391-23-S-2403
  • fa2391-23-s-2403
  • FA239123S2403

Dashes, spaces and letter case are all handled. So if you have typed the number correctly and still get nothing, stop re-typing it with different punctuation — that is not where the problem is.

What to do instead

1. Search the number anyway, then widen

It costs nothing and occasionally works — an amendment may still be open even when the version you were sent has closed. If it returns nothing, drop the number and search for what was being bought instead: the agency, the NAICS code, the PSC code, or a few words from the description. Recompetes are common, and the follow-on is often already posted.

2. Look the codes up if you do not know them

If you only know the work and not the codes, the NAICS code finder and PSC code finder both take a plain-English description and return the matching codes. From there you can browse everything currently open under that code.

3. Stop searching after the fact

This is the part that actually changes outcomes. With a 12-day median window, the only reliable way to see a solicitation while you can still bid on it is to be notified when it posts — not to hear about it and go looking.

Save a search for the NAICS codes, PSC codes, agencies or keywords that match what you do, and turn on alerts. New matching opportunities then arrive on day one of the window instead of day nine. Searching is free and needs no account; saving a search and enabling alerts needs a free account.

The short version

  • An empty result for a valid solicitation number almost always means it closed, not that you typed it wrong.
  • The median federal solicitation is open 12 days.
  • Closed notices are archived, not deleted, and are excluded from open-opportunity search.
  • Amendments get new notice IDs, so one solicitation can appear several times.
  • Searching by number is reactive. Alerts are the fix.
